# Collaboration Skills for Civic Life

Civic life depends on collaboration: meetings, roles, proposals, rules, shared responsibilities, public problems, disagreement, and community action. Collaboration helps kids practice how people work together — without pretending everyone always agrees.

This page is the local doorway into the Literacy for Kids [Collaboration Toolkit](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/collaboration), connected to the civic understanding this curriculum builds.

## A few core ideas

- **Civic work needs shared goals and visible roles.** Community projects are shared work at scale.
- **Disagreement can be part of civic life.** People can disagree about priorities and still keep building.
- **Groups need fair participation.** A fair process gives quieter voices a way in.
- **Repair matters when people feel unheard or excluded.** Civic groups have messes too.
- **Shared action needs follow-through.** Who is doing what, and by when?

## When this shows up

- When designing classroom or community rules
- When planning a proposal
- When assigning roles in a group
- When discussing public problems
- When people disagree about priorities
- When finishing and presenting a civic project

## Tools that help

- **Shared civic goal** — what problem is the group addressing?
- **Role checklist** — who researches, records, presents, and checks the goal.
- **Fair voice check** — has everyone had a way to weigh in?
- **Disagree and build frame** — "What part of our shared goal do we agree on?"
- **Finish together reflection** — what worked, who helped, what should change?

:::tip Collaboration Moment
Civic projects are shared work. Before the group starts, name the goal, the roles, and how people will check in. That helps the project become a team effort instead of one loud voice.
:::

:::caution Everyday collaboration skills, not unsafe conflict management
These are everyday collaboration tools, not therapy, bullying intervention, conflict mediation, legal advice, or a replacement for trusted adults, educators, caregivers, or qualified support. Kids should not be expected to manage unsafe, coercive, bullying, discriminatory, or threatening group situations alone. If group work feels unsafe, targeted, or too big, involve a trusted adult.
:::
