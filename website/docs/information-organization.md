# Information Organization Skills for Civic Life

Civic literacy includes rules, roles, rights, responsibilities, timelines, processes, public problems, proposals, and evidence. Information Organization helps kids make civic systems easier to understand.

This page is the local doorway into the Literacy for Kids [Information Organization Toolkit](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/information-organization), connected to the civic understanding this curriculum builds.

## A few core ideas

- **Civic systems often have many parts.** Grouping the parts makes the system visible.
- **Timelines help show order.** Processes make more sense as first-then-next-finally.
- **Role charts help show who does what.** Roles and responsibilities are information too.
- **Evidence tables help arguments stay clear.** Claims and support belong side by side.
- **Public problems need categories and questions** before they need answers.

## When this shows up

- When learning how a process works
- When sorting rules, roles, and responsibilities
- When building an argument
- When comparing public options
- When tracking a timeline
- When creating a civic proposal

## Tools that help

- **Rule / role / responsibility sort** — three piles for any system.
- **Civic process timeline** — first ___, then ___, next ___, finally ___.
- **Evidence table** — claim on one side, support on the other.
- **Public problem category map** — what kind of problem is each part?
- **Proposal notes** — evidence, sources, and open questions kept together.

:::tip Information Organization Moment
A civic process is easier to understand when the steps are in order. Try making a timeline: first ___, then ___, next ___, finally ___. Timelines help complicated systems become visible.
:::

:::caution Everyday organization skills, not a replacement for support
These are everyday information organization tools, not therapy, disability evaluation, academic intervention, legal advice, medical advice, or a replacement for trusted adults, educators, caregivers, accommodations, or qualified support. Kids should not be treated as lazy or broken when information feels messy or hard to organize. If information feels consistently overwhelming, inaccessible, unsafe, private, sensitive, or too big, involve a trusted adult and appropriate support.
:::
